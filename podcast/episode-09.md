# Episode 09: NEAT versus PPO, five projects later

This episode steps back from the daily numbers. You asked how NEAT compares with PPO, so let me tell the story of the projects so far, and what each one taught about these two ways of learning. There is also a short film on the site that goes through PPO step by step, with pictures.

First, the two methods in one sentence each. NEAT is evolution. It breeds a population of neural networks, keeps the ones that fly best, and lets their children change at random, even growing new neurons. PPO is reinforcement learning. It trains one network, and after every batch of flights it works out, step by step, which decisions helped and which hurt, and nudges every weight in that direction.

Now the projects. The very first lander, in calm air, was solved by NEAT with a network that had no hidden neurons at all. Simple task, simple answer. Then came wind. NEAT tried six different setups, including little memory loops, and the best reached eighty five percent. The next project added a curriculum and a cloned pilot as a head start. NEAT stalled again, at about eighty five. One lesson from there was harsh. NEAT's random changes kept damaging the good pilot it started from.

Then the last project tried PPO. It started with a fair contest. PPO from scratch, PPO from the cloned pilot, and an evolution method from the clone, each got forty five minutes of the whole computer. PPO from scratch won, with ninety one percent. The copied pilot barely improved. Then PPO practised its hardest cases. After about four and a half hours and two hundred and fifty million simulated steps, it passed two separate test sets, at ninety eight point eight and ninety eight point four percent.

So this project asked a fair question. Can NEAT get there too, if we do everything right? A clean start, a curriculum, a network big enough to grow, and a simulator five times faster. The answer so far is: closer than ever, not there yet. Its best pilot lands ninety three percent on a thousand flights. That is the best NEAT has ever done on this task, by eight points. But it took more than thirty billion simulated steps. That is over a hundred times what PPO needed.

Why such a gap? It comes down to how much each method learns from one flight. PPO writes down every step: what it saw, what it did, and what happened next. A second network, the critic, predicts how well things should go from each moment. Wherever reality beats that prediction, the action gets more likely. Wherever it falls short, less likely. So a single flight of two hundred steps gives two hundred small lessons, each pointing at a specific moment. NEAT gets one number per flight. Hundreds of decisions blur into one score, and that score is noisy. That is why this week we had to double the flights per network twice, from thirty two to a hundred and twenty eight. Each time, the noise had grown bigger than the improvements we were trying to see.

PPO has one more habit that matters. It moves in small steps. It refuses to change the pilot too much in one update, which is the proximal part of its name. That lets it reuse each batch of experience several times without wrecking what already works. Remember the NEAT project where random changes kept damaging a good pilot. PPO is built to avoid exactly that.

Is NEAT then just worse? Not quite. Its pilot uses forty one hidden neurons and a hundred and ten connections. PPO's has more than nineteen thousand weights. NEAT needs no calculus and no step-by-step rewards, only a final score, and it spreads easily over many computers. With thousands of processors, researchers have made evolution competitive with reinforcement learning. On one laptop with four cores, learning from every step wins.

What's next? Training is paused at generation four thousand eight hundred and ninety five, at your request. When it resumes, NEAT has a bit under three days to find the last two points. Either way, we now know the price of the one number per flight.
